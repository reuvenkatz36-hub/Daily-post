# The Biggest IPO in History: What SpaceX's $75B Listing Teaches About Investing

**Date:** 2026-06-06
**Topic:** SpaceX is about to pull off the largest IPO ever — a $75B raise at a ~$1.75 trillion valuation, despite losing money last year. It's a masterclass in how markets price the future.
**One-liner:** SpaceX lost $4.9B last year and is about to be valued near $1.75 trillion. Here's what that paradox teaches you about IPOs, valuations, and what a "share" really buys.

---

## STORY BREAKDOWN

### WHAT HAPPENED
- SpaceX is set to list on the **Nasdaq under ticker SPCX on June 12, 2026**, pricing June 11 after the roadshow that began June 4.
- It's targeting **~$135/share across ~556.6M shares — a ~$75 billion raise at roughly a $1.75 trillion valuation**, which would be the **largest IPO in history**. The deal is already reported oversubscribed.
- In 2025 the company reported **$18.7B in revenue and a $4.9B net loss** — so investors are buying the future, not current profits.
- The structure is unusual: **dual-class shares leave Elon Musk with ~85% of the voting power**, and a large **~30% slice is carved out for retail investors** via Robinhood, Fidelity, and Schwab.
- The valuation rests on three bets: **Starlink** (which generated ~$11.4B of 2025 revenue), **xAI / orbital AI compute**, and **Starship**.

### WHY IT MATTERS
- It's the clearest example in years of **valuation as a story about the future**, not a reflection of today's earnings.
- The **retail carve-out** is one of the largest ever — millions of ordinary investors can buy in at the IPO, not just institutions.
- The **dual-class structure** spotlights a question most people never ask: when you buy a share, how much *control* are you actually getting? (Here, almost none.)
- It concentrates enormous value on **one founder's vision** — a textbook case of key-person risk.
- Bank forecasts are sky-high (Morgan Stanley sees AI revenue near $190B by 2030; Goldman models total revenue topping $474B) — but **forecasts are not facts**.

### LESSONS FOR INVESTORS
- **A valuation is a forecast, not a fact.** A ~$1.75T price on a company that lost $4.9B last year means the market is paying for what *might* happen. Ask what has to go right to justify it.
- **Read what your share actually owns.** Dual-class stock can mean you have economic upside but virtually no vote. That's a trade-off, not a deal-breaker — but know it.
- **Hype and oversubscription aren't fundamentals.** "Oversubscribed" tells you about demand, not value. The best IPOs and the worst can both sell out.
- **Beware key-person risk.** When a story depends almost entirely on one person, you're underwriting them as much as the business.
- **You don't have to play.** Skipping an exciting IPO is a valid decision. There's no rule that you must own the famous name.

### LESSONS FOR ENTREPRENEURS
- **Narrative + numbers wins.** SpaceX pairs a bold vision with real revenue (Starlink). Vision raises capital; cash flow makes it credible.
- **Vertical integration is a moat.** Rockets, satellites, internet, and AI compute under one roof is hard to copy — control of the stack is strategic leverage.
- **Fund the moonshot with a cash cow.** Starlink's revenue underwrites the riskier bets. Build (or buy) a reliable engine before you chase the big one.
- **Structure your ownership early.** Founders who care about long-term control design share classes and voting rights *before* they need them.
- **Sequence your bets.** SpaceX layered Starlink → xAI → Starship. Stack credibility one proven step at a time instead of betting everything at once.

### MINDSET TAKEAWAY
> Price tells you what the crowd believes about the future. Your job isn't to guess the price — it's to decide whether you believe the story, and to know exactly what you own if you're wrong.

**SpaceX lost $4.9 billion last year. This week it's set to become the largest IPO in history, valued near $1.75 trillion. That contradiction is one of the best investing lessons you'll get all year.**

On June 12, SpaceX is expected to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, raising roughly $75 billion at about a $1.75 trillion valuation — the biggest public offering ever recorded, and reportedly already oversubscribed. Yet in 2025 the company posted $18.7 billion in revenue against a $4.9 billion net loss.

So how does a money-losing company command a trillion-dollar-plus price tag?

Because **a valuation isn't a measure of today's profit — it's a bet on tomorrow's.** Investors aren't paying for last year's income statement; they're paying for what SpaceX might become over the next decade. The price rests on three growth stories: Starlink, the satellite-internet business that already generated around $11.4 billion in 2025; xAI and the idea of AI compute in orbit; and Starship, which could lower the cost of launch dramatically. One is a real cash engine today. The other two are moonshots.

That framing leads to a few lessons worth keeping, whether or not you ever buy a single share:

**1. A valuation is a forecast, not a fact.** The most useful question before buying any exciting stock is: *what has to go right for this price to make sense?* If the honest answer is "nearly everything," you've just identified your risk.

**2. Know what your share actually owns.** SpaceX is using a dual-class structure that leaves Elon Musk with roughly 85% of the voting power. Public shareholders get economic exposure but almost no say in decisions. That's not necessarily bad — but it's something you should understand before, not after, you invest.

**3. Demand is not value.** "Oversubscribed" tells you a deal is popular. It tells you nothing about whether the price is reasonable. Some of history's hottest IPOs made fortunes; others erased them.

**4. Mind key-person risk.** When a company's entire thesis hinges on one founder's vision, you're underwriting the person as much as the enterprise.

For founders, SpaceX is also a strategy lesson. It funds its riskiest ambitions with a dependable cash cow (Starlink), it's vertically integrated across rockets, satellites, internet, and AI compute, and it sequenced its bets — proving one before chasing the next.

The point of all this isn't "buy" or "avoid." It's that **price reflects what the crowd believes about the future.** Your job is to decide whether you believe the story — and to know exactly what you own if you turn out to be wrong.
